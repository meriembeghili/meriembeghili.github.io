About

I examine what is at stake — ethically, relationally, and institutionally — when artificial entities enter care relationships.

I am completing a PhD at Sorbonne Université (ISIR–CNRS) on the ethical issues raised by the use of social robots in healthcare (defense expected December 2026), and I work as a Research Engineer at ISIR (CNRS) on INNOVCARE, an interdisciplinary project on care-led innovation for elderly autonomy.

My doctoral research led me to design a reusable ethical-risk assessment framework for AI systems and robots deployed in healthcare, applicable both to systems already in use and to prospective cases. My fieldwork in care settings — semi-structured interviews with clinicians, caregivers, and roboticists — surfaces risks that designers had not anticipated. I have led cross-cutting work with roboticists, clinicians, and project partners on two funded projects (INNOVCARE, TAPAS), including an international Franco-Japanese consortium.

This research has resulted in six peer-reviewed publications on trust, explainability, LLM-based conversational agents, and assistive robotics; invited and conference talks in three countries; and service on the programme committee of HHAI 2026. My interests now extend to AI governance and AI Act compliance.

Now: completing my PhD (defense December 2026) · open to roles in AI ethics, governance & compliance.

Research

Thesis. Ethical Issues in the Use of Social Robots in Healthcare — supervised by Anouk Barberousse and Mohamed Chetouani. Affiliations: Sciences, Normes, Décision (SND), Institut des Systèmes Intelligents et de Robotique (ISIR–CNRS), Sorbonne Center for Artificial Intelligence (SCAI).

The dissertation examines the ethical issues surrounding the introduction and deployment, into care relationships, of artificial entities designed to simulate a social presence — and demonstrates that they are morally incapable of being what our vocabulary suggests they are, a discrepancy that becomes a major ethical problem in this context.

01

Trust

Trust directed toward machines lacks the moral foundation that characterizes interpersonal trust — goodwill, reciprocity, affective responsiveness. This absence opens ethical risks in healthcare settings, where patients' vulnerability must be taken into account as a fundamental aspect of the caregiver–patient relationship.

02

Autonomy

A semantic drift whose reduced usages emerged in parallel in moral philosophy, biomedical ethics, geriatric medicine, and AI research and engineering. For the systems examined, what the word designates is heteronomy in Kant's sense: behavior is determined by objectives and constraints set from outside. The equivocation encourages the anthropomorphization of machines, the responsibility gap, and the dehumanization of care.

03

Risk Assessment

From theoretical analysis to practical assessment: an operational approach for evaluating the ethical risks associated with the use of social robots in healthcare settings, illustrated by two case studies (PARO and MediXpert, an imaginary robot).

The thesis does not challenge the deployment of social robots in healthcare settings, but instead calls for philosophical vigilance with respect to the language used to describe them: terms such as “trust”, “autonomy”, and “care” carry moral presuppositions that machines are, by their nature, incapable of satisfying. These risks are especially pronounced in care relationships, which are inherently defined by vulnerability and asymmetry.
